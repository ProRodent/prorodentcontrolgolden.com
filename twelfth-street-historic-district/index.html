Attics, basements and crawl spaces.

Houses in settled neighborhoods often have attics, basements or crawl spaces that go years without a visit. Rodents like those places because nobody disturbs them. If you can look in safely, check for droppings, flattened insulation and gnawed pipe wrap.

  • Bring a flashlight, wear gloves and use a mask rated for fine particles.
  • Leave nests alone rather than disturb them with bare hands.
  • Keep your head clear of low beams and sharp nails.
  • Hand anything unsafe over to the technician.

If you suspect heavy activity, skip the entry and ring. The technician brings gear and a plan.

From plan to quiet.

The inspection names the animal and lays out the route. Trapping removes the animals while the entries stay open, and sealing closes them once activity ends. Cleanup and repair come last.

A checklist by season.

  • Autumn: inspect door seals, vent covers and the roofline before the first hard cold.
  • Winter: listen at night and note where sounds come from.
  • Spring: look for new gaps once the thaw is done.
  • Summer: trim branches that touch the roof and watch outdoor pet food.

Safety first.

Droppings carry dust that you should not breathe. Never sweep, vacuum or blow them. Open the space, wait about thirty minutes, wet the mess with disinfectant and lift it up with paper towels. The full CDC steps appear in the safety box below.

Steps for today.

  • Steer pets and children away from any room showing signs.
  • Put gloves on before handling anything stained, chewed or damp.
  • Sticky boards also snare lizards, birds and house pets, so keep them on the shelf.
  • Take photos of droppings and gnaw marks before anyone tidies.

Safe cleanup in six brief points

A few minutes of reading now keeps dust out of the air later. The points follow the agency's own sequence.

  • Skip brooms and vacuums entirely. They throw dry droppings into the air you breathe, so they stay in the closet.
  • Before you step into a cabin, shed, attic or garage that has been shut for ages, let fresh air flow through for thirty minutes.
  • Hands carry waste to faces, so gloves go on first and a careful wash comes last.
  • Soak the mess through and leave it around five minutes, so the dust cannot rise.
  • Lift a dead rodent into a bag with a gloved hand or a tool, then seal it together with the used towels before the bin.
  • Illness after a cleanup belongs with a doctor and not an exterminator. Describe the job to a physician.
