Food businesses nearby.

Where restaurants, bars and cafes cluster, rodents meet plenty of scraps, cardboard and bins. A home or shop close to that activity may feel the pressure, because animals follow walls and drains. We cannot say how many food businesses stand near you, so watch your own bins and doors and compare notes with neighbors.

  • Keep lids on trash cans snug and wipe spills around them.
  • Bring pet bowls inside overnight.
  • Plug gaps where pipes or cables pass through walls.
  • Mention rodent activity near a shared dumpster to the nearest manager.

Shared walls and rear alleys.

Dense blocks tend to have common walls, rear alleys and shared service lines. A gap in one unit can supply the next, which makes cooperation worthwhile. Renters should ask the landlord to permit a check of adjoining spaces, because sealing only your side leaves half the route open.

Our sealing page describes the metals used at pipe entries and door corners, which are weak points in old and new buildings alike.

Cold snaps and thaw.

A sudden freeze sends animals toward heated buildings, and repeated freezing and thawing can open hairline cracks at doors and foundations. Once the first hard freeze has passed, stroll around your building and note any new gap, loose vent cover or debris piled against a wall.

How a visit runs.

The technician begins outdoors, then examines the attic or ceiling space, the storeroom and any kitchen or utility room. Droppings, grease marks and gnawing reveal the routes. Traps go along them, and with the building empty, metal and mesh close the gaps.

A written plan follows, with each stage on its own line, and a return trip confirms that the traps stay quiet and the seals hold.

While you wait for the visit.

Let droppings lie. Should you clean anyway, use the wet method from the safety box below, photograph what you find, keep children and pets away and pass on loose bait.

Small jobs before the visit.

  • Quiet the room, then listen from one spot and note where the sound seems strongest.
  • Skip foggers and sprays that only drive animals deeper into the structure.
  • Check that the garage door seal lies flat against the slab.
  • Photograph anything in daylight that resembles an entry gap.

Keep the dust down with this short list

Start with this list, then decide which tasks are yours and which to hand off.

  • Never dry-sweep, dry-vacuum or blow waste around with a leaf blower. Stirred dust is how people get exposed.
  • Give a closed room time to breathe: open doors and windows, then stay out for about half an hour.
  • Hands carry waste to faces, so gloves go on first and a careful wash comes last.
  • Drench droppings and nests, and the surface under them, with disinfectant or a bleach mix. Let it work for about five minutes.
  • Take up the wet waste with paper towels, drop them in a plastic bag along with any dead animal, tie it shut and put it in the trash.
  • Flu-like symptoms after contact with rodent waste are a medical matter, so tell your physician about the exposure.
