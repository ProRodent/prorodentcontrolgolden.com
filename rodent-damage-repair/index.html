Why repair comes last.

Fixing damage before the animals are gone only invites more. That is why repair ends the chain: inspection first, then trapping, sealing, cleanup and finally repair.

The same logic governs cost. A repaired attic that is not sealed will be chewed again, and the second bill is the one nobody wanted.

Damage we often meet.

  • Insulation compressed, torn or soiled in the attic, crawl space or walls.
  • Duct wrap or flexible duct chewed through.
  • Window and vent screens with holes.
  • Door sweeps, weatherstripping and garage seals gnawed.
  • Wood trim, soffit corners and plastic pipe with bite marks.
  • Stored items such as bins, fabric and paper.

Some of this is cosmetic and some affects comfort or safety. The technician explains which is which.

Work we leave alone.

Chewed wiring is a fire risk, and we do not repair it. If you see bare conductors or a jacket bitten through, stay away and phone an electrician. Large drywall holes, roof leaks and structural damage also go to other trades.

If a rodent has damaged something that carries a hazard, such as a gas line, tell the technician at once and leave the area.

Older and newer finishes.

We do not know the age of your house and do not assume it. The repair list depends on what rodents damaged and not on a guess about the building. Insulation types vary widely, and the technician identifies yours at the walk-through.

Making repairs last.

A repair lasts while the entries stay closed. After the work, check the seals twice a year, and see the sealing page for what to watch. If a smell appears after a repair, odor control may be needed.

On your side of the door.

  • Bag food with bite marks before it goes in the bin.
  • Keep the outdoor trash lid tight tonight.
  • Hold off sealing a hole if you suspect an animal is still inside.
  • Tell the dispatcher about anyone at home with asthma or a weakened immune system.

Keep the dust down with this short list

Bookmark these points for any room, shed or cabin where droppings appear.

  • Avoid any method that raises dust, which rules out brooms, shop vacs and compressed air near droppings.
  • Open what you can, walk outside and wait roughly thirty minutes while stale air clears.
  • Disposable gloves of rubber, vinyl or latex belong on your hands for anything soiled, and a thorough soap-and-water wash belongs at the end.
  • Soak the mess through and leave it around five minutes, so the dust cannot rise.
  • Lift a dead rodent into a bag with a gloved hand or a tool, then seal it together with the used towels before the bin.
  • Flu-like symptoms after contact with rodent waste are a medical matter, so tell your physician about the exposure.

Repairs: what people ask.

Is wiring repaired?

No. If we see chewed wiring we report it and suggest a licensed electrician. Never touch bare wires.

What about drywall or roofing?

Large drywall or roof repairs belong to other trades. We handle small rodent-related patches and pass on bigger jobs.

What gets damaged most?

Insulation, duct wrap, screens, door sweeps, wood trim, plastic pipe and stored items are common targets.

Will insurance pay?

Policies vary, and many leave rodent damage out. Ask your insurer first, and request photos and a written estimate from us to back up any claim.

When can repairs begin?

Once activity has stopped and entries are shut. The technician tells you when that point arrives.
