The yard is the starting line.

Most rodent trouble indoors begins outside. Animals live in burrows, brush and wood stacks and travel to the building for food and warmth. Cutting the route outdoors lowers the pressure on your walls.

Golden lots differ widely. Some lie on level streets near downtown, and others climb toward Lookout Mountain and the Table Mountains, with rock, scrub and open ground next door. Each offers different cover, and the technician tailors the plan.

Outdoor checkpoints.

  • Burrow holes along foundations, slab edges, fences and the base of sheds.
  • Wood stacks, lumber and stored materials piled against walls.
  • Feeders, pet bowls, compost bins and loose trash cans.
  • Dense shrubs, vines and ground cover touching the siding.
  • Branches that reach the roof or gutters.
  • Fence gates, shed doors and any gap beneath them.

The walk takes less time than most people expect, because the technician is reading patterns and not individual leaves.

From yard to building.

Outdoor work and sealing belong together. A tidy yard with a gap in the foundation still lets animals in. Our sealing page describes how wall and roofline openings are closed, and the rat control page covers what happens when burrows are active.

Creek corridors and drainage channels often bring moisture and cover. We do not claim they cause trouble at any given address, but they are worth a mention if your property borders one.

Sheds, decks and outbuildings.

A shed with a gap beneath the floor makes an ideal nest. Decks create dark space beside the house, and a wood step touching the siding can serve as a ladder. Each fix is a small project, and the technician lists the ones worth doing.

An outbuilding that holds hay, feed, firewood or birdseed should be treated as part of the house. Check it as often as you check the garage.

Watching through the seasons.

Choose a weekend each season and tour the lot with a notepad. Tally burrows, note fresh droppings and check the base of every outbuilding. A tally that doubles between walks means something is changing.

Keep dated notes. If you later phone for a technician, a short log saves time on the first visit.

Between the call and the arrival.

  • Bag food with bite marks before it goes in the bin.
  • Keep the outdoor trash lid tight tonight.
  • Hold off sealing a hole if you suspect an animal is still inside.
  • Tell the dispatcher about anyone at home with asthma or a weakened immune system.

Handling the mess the CDC way

The CDC steps appear here in plain words so you need not hunt for them.

  • Avoid any method that raises dust, which rules out brooms, shop vacs and compressed air near droppings.
  • Open what you can, walk outside and wait roughly thirty minutes while stale air clears.
  • Hands carry waste to faces, so gloves go on first and a careful wash comes last.
  • Wet everything thoroughly with a household disinfectant or diluted bleach, and let it stand a few minutes before wiping.
  • Take up the wet waste with paper towels, drop them in a plastic bag along with any dead animal, tie it shut and put it in the trash.
  • Illness after a cleanup belongs with a doctor and not an exterminator. Describe the job to a physician.

Yard and outdoor questions.

Do feeders draw rats?

Spilled seed can. A tray under the feeder, sweeping every day or taking the feeder down for a season all shrink the supply.

Is a wood stack a problem?

One against the siding is. Raise it on a rack and move it several feet from the building.

What about backyard chickens?

Feed and eggs attract rodents. Keep feed in a metal bin with a tight lid and clear spills each evening.

Should I fill in burrows?

Not until you know they are empty. Packing soil into an active burrow just pushes animals to dig a fresh exit.

Does thick ground cover help rodents?

It gives cover beside a foundation. Keep a hand's width of bare soil or gravel between plants and the wall.
