Ground the inspection covers.

This kind of inspection is a methodical search that follows a set order. The technician begins in the room where you noticed trouble, moves through the rest of the building and ends outdoors.

  • Interior hot spots: pantry, sink bases, behind appliances and closets on exterior walls.
  • Attic, crawl space and basement, including vents, hatches and pipe entries.
  • Garage doors, thresholds, shelving and stored goods.
  • Roofline, siding, foundation, window wells and utility penetrations.
  • Yard features such as sheds, wood stacks, feeders and compost, plus anything touching the house.

Findings are noted as the walk proceeds, and the technician explains what each suggests. You are welcome to follow along.

Turning clues into a plan.

Droppings are the most common clue, and size and shape suggest a species. Chew marks, grease smears and prints in dust add detail. Insulation torn into a bowl suggests a nest, and dark streaks along a wall suggest a route.

The plan then arranges the work: which animals, which traps, which gaps and which cleanup. Sealing follows once trapping is working, so nothing is shut inside a wall.

How the estimate reads.

Once the walk ends, the plan reaches you in writing. Every line names a task, the area it covers and the price. The four kinds of work stay separate, which means a yes to one carries no obligation toward the rest.

Prices describe the work listed, and nothing is added without your approval. If something new appears once work starts, you hear about it before the bill changes.

Until a technician arrives.

  • If you must enter a dusty space, wear a mask rated for fine particles.
  • Keep fans off in rooms with droppings, since moving air lifts dust.
  • Leave closets and cabinets with gnawing untouched until the inspection.
  • List what the animals damaged, with photos.

Safe cleanup in six brief points

The CDC steps appear here in plain words so you need not hunt for them.

  • Avoid any method that raises dust, which rules out brooms, shop vacs and compressed air near droppings.
  • Open what you can, walk outside and wait roughly thirty minutes while stale air clears.
  • Wear disposable gloves throughout and wash with soap and running water when they come off.
  • Wet everything thoroughly with a household disinfectant or diluted bleach, and let it stand a few minutes before wiping.
  • Take up the wet waste with paper towels, drop them in a plastic bag along with any dead animal, tie it shut and put it in the trash.
  • Fever, aches or shortness of breath within a few weeks of cleaning calls for a doctor. Say what you handled. A pest company cannot advise on health.

Inspections: what people ask.

How long will a walk-through last?

It varies with the building and the evidence. A small home can take under an hour, while a big lot with outbuildings runs longer. The dispatcher gives a rough window.

Will the technician enter cramped spaces?

Accessible attics and crawl spaces get entered, and any area that is unsafe or unreachable is reported to you. Clear a path to the hatch beforehand.

Must I stay home?

Someone needs to open up and answer the phone. We ask for access to the building, not for you to watch.

What if there are no rodents?

You are told so plainly, along with any gaps worth closing before animals discover them.

Do you inspect cabins and second homes?

Yes, when the address falls within our range. Say how long the building has stood closed.

Where an inspection can lead.

  • Business sites.

    A short written record after each visit serves owners, managers and any tenants who share walls or bins.

  • Trapping.

    Device type depends on the species and on who lives in the home, with each recheck dated ahead of time.

  • Sealing.

    This is the half that lasts. Each gap is located, sized up and covered with metal beyond the reach of teeth.
