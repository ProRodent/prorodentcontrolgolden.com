Cold snaps and thaw.

A building left empty through winter or summer is a prime target. Clear out food, close gaps and ask a neighbor to look in now and then.

Sounds, odors and leftovers.

These are the clues people report most. One alone proves little, yet three together seldom point elsewhere.

  • Rustling in the walls when the furnace kicks on.
  • Droppings in drawers, cupboards or a pantry.
  • Bite marks on stored boxes or bin lids.
  • Seed or grain scattered across the garage.
  • A trail of tiny paw prints in dust.

Holding steps.

  • Stay out of attics and crawl spaces until the animals are dealt with.
  • Stack nothing fresh against the walls of the affected room.
  • Wash with soap after handling anything a rodent could have touched.
  • Lay a coin next to droppings in photos so their size shows.

Do and do not, for rodent waste

The CDC steps appear here in plain words so you need not hunt for them.

  • Never dry-sweep, dry-vacuum or blow waste around with a leaf blower. Stirred dust is how people get exposed.
  • Give a closed room time to breathe: open doors and windows, then stay out for about half an hour.
  • Wear disposable gloves throughout and wash with soap and running water when they come off.
  • Drench droppings and nests, and the surface under them, with disinfectant or a bleach mix. Let it work for about five minutes.
  • Lift a dead rodent into a bag with a gloved hand or a tool, then seal it together with the used towels before the bin.
  • Illness after a cleanup belongs with a doctor and not an exterminator. Describe the job to a physician.
