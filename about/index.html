The first minute of a call.

The person on the line wants four answers: the address, the symptom, the timeline and whether kids or animals share the space. From those, the right technician and the right gear get chosen.

Nights and holidays work the same way. If no one can drive out today, you are told so before you hang up, together with a window you can plan around.

Order of work at the property.

  • Circle the roofline first, then probe each attic, garage, crawl space and yard corner for recent signs.
  • Set traps or stations where the signs form a lane.
  • Cap each hole using flashing, steel mesh and sealant that cures hard.
  • Come back to tally catches, push on the repairs and arrange cleanup if needed.

That sequence is on purpose. Sealing early may entomb a live animal, while cleaning a space that still has open doors only means cleaning it again later.

Details you will not find here.

You will not see a founding year, a license number, staff portraits, trophies or glowing quotes here. We hold nothing of the kind in a form we can prove, and a made-up detail does more harm than a gap. Questions about credentials are best put to the technician in person.

We also avoid swearing that rodents will stay away for good. Hillsides, creek banks, wood stacks and the neighbor's yard keep a supply of animals close by. What you get instead is a careful search, durable metal and a return trip.

Range and the listed address.

Eligibility turns on the ZIP code. Our data gives Golden three, and the areas page adds codes from fourteen other places. Denver is reachable only in part, so its page lists just the codes that qualify.

The footer of every page lists 4657 12th Street, Golden, CO 80403. That is a directory line and not a shop front, so nothing is sold there. Bookings come by phone and the work takes place at your address.

Bait, health and where to ask.

Federal rules limit how certain rodent poisons are sold and used, and the EPA page below describes them. Our approach leans on traps plus sealing. Should bait ever be part of a job, you would learn the product and its location.

Cleaning up waste follows the CDC order: ventilate, soak, wipe, bag. For medical worries, see your own physician, since rodents are the only subject we can address.

If the signs above match yours, the next move is a conversation, and the line is open now.

Who shows up.

A dispatcher does the scheduling, and a technician performs the hands-on work at your property. No staff names or photos are published, and we claim no certificate that we cannot document. If you wonder who is coming, ask while booking.

Work beyond rodent control, such as roofing, electrical fixes and large drywall jobs, goes to other trades, and the walk-through will say when one is needed.

How we describe findings.

Reports use plain words. If a technician finds droppings, the note says where, how fresh they looked and what animal they suggest. If nothing is found, the note says that too, and any gap worth closing is listed beside it. You keep a copy of every note.

When a finding is uncertain, we say it is uncertain. A guess dressed up as a fact would send you toward the wrong repair, and the whole point of the visit is to avoid that.

Prices and timing.

Price comes after the walk-through, since a figure given sight unseen is a guess. The estimate lists sealing, cleanup and repair individually, so you can take or leave each in turn.

Same-day arrival depends on how full the calendar is. You are told what is open as you call, and no promise is made that the schedule cannot keep.
