Picturing your property.

We know Wheat Ridge through its ZIP codes and type of place and not through its streets, so we do not describe your block. We ask about fences, the garage, any detached shed and whether trees or shrubs touch the house.

We question the lot before assuming anything. Does a fence meet the house? Is there a shed, a detached garage, a deck with a dark underside or a wood stack by the wall? The technician begins in a different place depending on each reply.

Our order of operations.

The visit follows a fixed order. We inspect and map the routes, set traps along them and then close gaps with steel and sealant. Sealing follows trapping so no animal is shut inside a wall. Cleanup and repair are saved for after the animals have left and every gap is shut.

Clues that add up.

Certainty is not required. Describe what you see and the dispatcher helps you judge how urgent it is.

  • Droppings on a window sill or a stair.
  • Cardboard shredded behind appliances.

Cold snaps and thaw.

A building left empty through winter or summer is a prime target. Clear out food, close gaps and ask a neighbor to look in now and then.

Fences, garages and the back lot.

On many residential lots the back fence, the garage and a shed form a sheltered strip where animals travel unseen. If your lot has one, tell us which side of the house it runs along, and the walk-through begins there.

Garage doors deserve a look of their own. A seal that no longer reaches the slab, a side door that sags and shelves shoved against the wall all help a mouse settle in, and each is easy to check.

Holding steps.

  • Bag food with bite marks before it goes in the bin.
  • Keep the outdoor trash lid tight tonight.
  • Hold off sealing a hole if you suspect an animal is still inside.
  • Tell the dispatcher about anyone at home with asthma or a weakened immune system.

Dust and droppings: ground rules first

Read these six points before touching a pellet, nest or stain. They come from the CDC and not from our opinion.

  • Avoid any method that raises dust, which rules out brooms, shop vacs and compressed air near droppings.
  • Give a closed room time to breathe: open doors and windows, then stay out for about half an hour.
  • Hands carry waste to faces, so gloves go on first and a careful wash comes last.
  • Drench droppings and nests, and the surface under them, with disinfectant or a bleach mix. Let it work for about five minutes.
  • Collect the soaked material on paper towels, seal it in a bag and throw it out with the household garbage.
  • Fever, aches or shortness of breath within a few weeks of cleaning calls for a doctor. Say what you handled. A pest company cannot advise on health.
