Placement decides the result.

A store-bought trap in mid-floor rarely catches anything. Mice and rats follow edges and slip through gaps, so traps belong against walls, along beams and at the mouth of a hole. The inspection shows where those spots are.

You pick between two devices, snap traps and enclosed stations. A snap trap works fast and leaves nothing to leak. Enclosed stations shield other animals from bait and suit outbuildings and some outdoor spots.

How a trapping job unfolds.

  • Day one: devices go onto the evidence lanes and each is entered on the log sheet.
  • A few days on: check, empty and relocate any trap that saw no traffic.
  • Second check: confirm that catches are tapering off.
  • Last step: seal the openings once activity has stopped.

Sealing early risks locking an animal in a wall, while waiting too long lets new animals find the same door. The log sheet keeps the timing honest.

Why poison is not our opener.

A poisoned rodent seldom drops beside the bait. It often wanders into a wall void or crawl space and dies there. The smell can last weeks, and the carcass is costly to reach. Bait also endangers pets and the hawks and owls that eat rodents.

The EPA limits how some rodenticide products are sold and used. Read its page in the sources box before buying anything for the garage.

Mountain and creekside lots.

Properties beside open slope, creek banks or woodlots see steady traffic from outside, which makes trapping a repeated effort instead of a single visit. After the first round, the best defense is sealing the entry points so newcomers meet metal.

If you share a wall or lot line with a neighbor who has rodents, mention it. We may suggest a word with the property manager or a joint plan.

Until a technician arrives.

  • Quiet the room, then listen from one spot and note where the sound seems strongest.
  • Skip foggers and sprays that only drive animals deeper into the structure.
  • Check that the garage door seal lies flat against the slab.
  • Photograph anything in daylight that resembles an entry gap.

Dust and droppings: ground rules first

Airborne dust is the hazard with rodent waste, so the order of steps matters more than speed.

  • Never dry-sweep, dry-vacuum or blow waste around with a leaf blower. Stirred dust is how people get exposed.
  • Open what you can, walk outside and wait roughly thirty minutes while stale air clears.
  • Wear disposable gloves throughout and wash with soap and running water when they come off.
  • Soak the mess through and leave it around five minutes, so the dust cannot rise.
  • Take up the wet waste with paper towels, drop them in a plastic bag along with any dead animal, tie it shut and put it in the trash.
  • Fever, aches or shortness of breath within a few weeks of cleaning calls for a doctor. Say what you handled. A pest company cannot advise on health.

Trapping: what people ask.

Is poison part of the plan?

We lean toward traps paired with sealing. Bait can kill animals inside walls and may harm pets and wildlife. Product limits are laid out on the EPA page in the sources box.

Do you use glue boards?

Not by choice. They snare lizards, birds and other bystanders, and they are miserable for everyone involved.

What becomes of the catch?

Animals go into a sealed bag for disposal, with handling that follows CDC advice on gloves and washing.

How many trips are needed?

A typical plan has an initial placement and one or two checks. The number depends on how many animals are active, and the dispatcher states it at the start.

Can traps go in an attic or crawl space?

Yes, whenever the space is safe to enter. The technician says so if it is not.
