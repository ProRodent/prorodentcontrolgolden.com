Garage Gear Storage and Rodents in Golden, CO

Colorado garages tend to hold a lot of seasonal gear. Here is how to store it so mice find it less appealing, and where to look when you pull it out again.

Open a Colorado garage in October and you will usually find the same things: skis in a box, a cooler, a camp stove, bike gear, a few totes and a pile of pet food. Everything is soft, hollow or both, and all of it sits for months.

To a mouse, that is a hotel. This guide covers how to store gear so it is less attractive, and what to check when you take it out again.

Why garages suit mice.

A garage offers quiet, shelter and plenty of nesting material. The door seal wears out, the side door sags and the slab meets the wall at a crack. A mouse needs only one of those.

We hold no data on how many Golden garages see rodents, and we will not make up a number. The reasoning is general: a garage with a gap, food and cover is a candidate.

Storing gear the smart way.

Item.Risk.Better storage.
Skis and boots in a fabric bag.Fabric shreds easily.Hard case or lidded tote.
Camp stove and cookware.Food residue.Scrub thoroughly, then seal in a tote.
Sleeping bags and pads.Soft nesting material.Plastic tote with a tight lid, off the floor.
Coolers.Crumbs inside.Clean, dry and stored with the lid slightly open.
Pet food and seed.A direct food source.Metal or heavy plastic bin with a tight lid.
Cardboard boxes.Easy to chew.Swap for plastic, or place on a shelf.

Leave a few inches between each item and the wall so droppings show up. Use shelves where you can, because the floor is where mice travel.

Inspect the door and the slab.

  • Check the bottom seal of the garage door for gaps or chewed rubber.
  • Look at every corner of the door for daylight.
  • Sweep a flashlight along the foot of the wall where the slab meets it.
  • Examine the side door, its threshold and its frame.
  • Check where wires and pipes pass through the wall.

A new door seal costs little, and it closes one of the most common routes. More on closing gaps is on the sealing page.

When you pull gear out.

Before you open a tote or ski box, look over the outside for chewed edges. Lift the lid slowly. If you see droppings, stop and follow the wet-cleaning steps in the safety box below.

  • Wear gloves.
  • Raise the garage door and let air move for about thirty minutes.
  • Photograph droppings and nesting material.
  • Dampen them with disinfectant, take them up and bag them.
  • Wash the gear before it comes into the house.

A few cautions.

Never sweep a dusty garage floor. If you must clear it, wet it first. If you find a nest, keep bare hands off it. And if you ever notice droppings in a closed shed, read the CDC hantavirus page in the sources box before doing anything else.

Through the seasons.

  • Fall: seal the door, store gear in plastic and clear out food.
  • Winter: check the garage monthly for droppings.
  • Spring: pull gear out slowly and inspect each item.
  • Summer: keep the door closed when not in use and watch pet food.

If you find activity, trapping and the garage and basement service cover the next steps.

A shelf plan that works.

Use one wall of the garage as a storage wall and keep it organized. Heavy lidded bins go on the lowest shelf, lighter gear above, and nothing touches the wall itself. That layout lets you spot droppings at a glance and sweep a light along the gap.

Label each bin with the season. When you swap gear in spring and fall, you open every lid, which doubles as a quick inspection twice a year.

Handling the mess the CDC way

Bookmark these points for any room, shed or cabin where droppings appear.

  • Avoid any method that raises dust, which rules out brooms, shop vacs and compressed air near droppings.
  • Open what you can, walk outside and wait roughly thirty minutes while stale air clears.
  • Hands carry waste to faces, so gloves go on first and a careful wash comes last.
  • Soak the mess through and leave it around five minutes, so the dust cannot rise.
  • Collect the soaked material on paper towels, seal it in a bag and throw it out with the household garbage.
  • Flu-like symptoms after contact with rodent waste are a medical matter, so tell your physician about the exposure.

Official sources worth a look

Agency pages are updated more often than any local web page, so treat them as the last word.

Before you go.

Are plastic totes truly better than cardboard?

Yes. Cardboard is easy to chew and shred for nests, while a lidded plastic tote is much harder to enter.

Can mice chew through plastic?

They can, especially thin plastic. Heavy-duty totes with tight lids hold up better.

Should I wash gear before storing it?

Cleaning off food residue helps a great deal. Camp stoves, coolers and packs often hold crumbs.

How far from the wall should gear sit?

A few inches, so you can see droppings and run a light behind it.

What about a garage freezer?

Keep the area around it clean and check behind it now and then. Warmth and stored food draw animals.
