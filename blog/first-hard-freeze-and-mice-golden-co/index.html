The First Hard Freeze and Mice in Golden, CO

The first night below freezing changes what mice do. Here is why, where to look before the cold arrives, and an honest note on how little a homeowner can judge from the curb.

Anyone who has lived along the Front Range knows how fast the weather turns. A mild October afternoon can give way to a night far below freezing. Wild animals sense the shift before people do, and some of them head for the nearest warm building.

Mice are often first through the door. A house mouse needs little: a gap about as thick as a pencil, a dry spot to nest and something edible within a short dash. This guide shows how to check all three before the cold sets in.

Why a cold snap shifts the balance.

Heat is the main lure. Every seam of a heated house leaks a trickle of warmth, and a mouse can pick that up at a door sweep or a vent. The animal that spent August under a wood stack may be testing your foundation by November.

Food counts too. Cold weather thins out what is available outdoors, which makes pet food, birdseed, pantry staples and the crumbs under a stove far more valuable. One open bag of dog food in a garage can keep a mouse close all winter.

We do not claim that any single street in Golden has more of this than another. We keep no counts, and an honest gap beats an invented figure. The pattern itself is common wherever winters are cold.

An autumn lap around the outside.

Choose a dry afternoon and circle the whole house with a flashlight, even by day. Look low first, along the foundation, and then work upward toward the roof.

  • Foundation: cracks, gaps at the sill plate and holes at the foot of the siding.
  • Vents: crawl space screens, dryer vent flaps and bath fan covers, pressing each to see if it wobbles.
  • Utility entries: pipes, gas lines and cables, checking for a gap around each.
  • Garage: the bottom seal, every corner of the door and the side entry.
  • Roof edge: soffits, fascia and the spots where a chimney or dormer meets the roof.
  • Contact points: branches, trellises and steps touching the house.

Mark each spot with painter's tape or a photo. A list of ten small defects is far easier to fix than a vague hunch that something is wrong.

What freezing and thawing do to gaps.

Water seeps into tiny cracks, freezes and expands, then thaws and leaves the crack a bit wider. Over one winter a hairline in masonry or siding can grow into a mouse-sized door. Sealant also stretches and shrinks with temperature, and old sealant may peel away from its edges.

That is the reason for a second walk. Look again after the spring thaw, because gaps opened by winter are the ones that matter next autumn.

Listening from indoors.

Sounds tell you something even when the animal stays hidden. Scratching low inside a wall suggests mice working along a stud bay. Light patter overhead points to the attic. Squeaks are common when young mice are present.

Jot down the room, the hour and the duration. A quick note on your phone is plenty, and those three facts speed a phone call far more than a long description.

  • Pause beside the pantry, near the water heater closet and along any garage wall shared with the kitchen.
  • Look at the backs of drawers and cabinets for pellets.
  • Check the oven drawer and beneath the refrigerator.
  • Peek at the lowest pantry shelf, where bagged and boxed goods sit.

Things to avoid.

Do not sweep up droppings. The CDC advises dampening them with disinfectant and wiping them up, since dry sweeping lifts dust into the air. The safety box below spells out the steps, and it is worth reading before any cleaning.

Stay away from loose bait. A mouse that eats poison frequently dies inside a wall, and the odor may linger for weeks. The EPA page in the sources list explains why some products are restricted.

Skip sticky boards if pets or wildlife live nearby, because they trap lizards and birds as readily as mice.

An afternoon in three installments.

  • Week one: tour the outside with a flashlight and photograph every gap.
  • Week two: close what you can with metal mesh and sealant, or book help for the rest.
  • Week three: move pet food and seed into lidded tubs and swap cardboard for plastic.
  • After the thaw: walk again and fix whatever winter opened.

A professional inspection is wise if you find more than a handful of gaps, hear sounds at night or cannot tell which defects matter. Sealing work closes the openings in metal.

Handling the mess the CDC way

Whether you take on the mess yourself or hand it to the technician, this order keeps particles out of your lungs.

  • Never dry-sweep, dry-vacuum or blow waste around with a leaf blower. Stirred dust is how people get exposed.
  • Before you step into a cabin, shed, attic or garage that has been shut for ages, let fresh air flow through for thirty minutes.
  • Wear disposable gloves throughout and wash with soap and running water when they come off.
  • Wet everything thoroughly with a household disinfectant or diluted bleach, and let it stand a few minutes before wiping.
  • Collect the soaked material on paper towels, seal it in a bag and throw it out with the household garbage.
  • Fever, aches or shortness of breath within a few weeks of cleaning calls for a doctor. Say what you handled. A pest company cannot advise on health.

Open the original pages

Common follow-ups.

Do mice truly move in at the first freeze?

Many go looking for warmth as nights turn cold, and a heated house with an open gap is inviting. Mice can come in during any other month too.

When should I inspect my house?

Late summer or early fall works best, ahead of the first hard freeze. A second look after the thaw catches gaps that opened during winter.

May I stuff a gap with steel wool?

As filler behind metal mesh, yes. Alone it can be dragged out, and it rusts when wet, so it should never be the only barrier.

How small a gap deserves closing?

About a quarter inch. A mouse can pass through a space roughly as thick as a pencil.

Is scratching enough reason to phone?

Yes. Note the room, the hour and how long it lasts, then tell the dispatcher.
