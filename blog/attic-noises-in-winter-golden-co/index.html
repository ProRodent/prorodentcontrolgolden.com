Attic Noises in Winter: What Golden Homeowners Hear

Noise overhead on a cold night is hard to ignore and harder to diagnose. This guide helps you separate rodent sounds from house sounds, and shows what to note before you phone.

Posted August 21, 2026, about 4 minutes to read.
Staffed 24/7.

Two in the morning, and something is shifting over your ceiling. Is it a mouse, a rat, the furnace or the house itself? In winter, with the heat on and the wind up, plenty of sounds can fool you.

This guide helps you sort them out. It also lists what to write down before you call, so the phone conversation stays short and useful.

Sounds and what they suggest.

What you hear.Likely cause.Remarks.
Light scratching or patter.Mice.Often at night, moving along beams and walls.
Heavy steps and scrabbling.Rats.Louder, sometimes with thumps or dragging.
Ticking or popping.Wood or metal expanding and contracting.Tied to temperature changes, not to movement across the room.
Rattling that follows the furnace.Ductwork.Usually repeats on a cycle.
Chirping or fluttering.Birds.Often during the day.
Squeaking.Young mice or rats.A sign that a nest may be near.

None of these is a guarantee. One sound could have several causes, but a pattern that repeats night after night suggests an animal.

What to write down.

  • Which room or ceiling the sound seems to be above.
  • When it begins and how long it goes on.
  • Whether it follows the heating cycle.
  • Whether you have seen droppings, gnawing or nesting material.
  • Whether any exterior gap, loose vent or damaged soffit might explain it.

A note on your phone is enough. The dispatcher uses those details to judge how urgent the call is and what to check first.

Evidence you can check safely.

Sound is only one clue. Look in the rooms below the noise for stains, odors or droppings in closets. From outside, check the roof edge and soffits for gaps. If a vent cover is loose or a screen is torn, take a photo.

Entering an attic can be hazardous. Insulation hides joists, wires and nails, and dust may be present. If you go in, wear gloves and a mask, step only on solid framing and keep the hatch open for air. If anything feels unsafe, leave it to the technician.

Dust and droppings.

Droppings in an attic can be dry and dusty. The CDC says not to sweep or vacuum them. Dampen them with disinfectant, give it a few minutes, then lift them away and bag them. The safety box below lists the steps.

Large amounts of waste or soiled insulation call for attic cleanup after the animals are gone.

What a visit involves.

  • The technician checks the roofline, vents and soffits for gaps.
  • The attic and any crawl space are inspected for droppings, nests and routes.
  • Traps go along the lanes the evidence shows.
  • Openings are closed in metal after the activity stops.
  • A return visit confirms that the noise has ended.

The inspection page explains how the written estimate is organized, and exclusion covers the metals used to close gaps.

Winter habits that help.

  • Keep branches trimmed off the roof.
  • Check the attic hatch seal.
  • Store pet food and seed in sealed bins.
  • Listen on a cold night and write down what you hear.
  • Look at the roofline after a heavy snow melts.

If you hear sounds every night for more than a few days, phone. Mice multiply quickly, and the first week is the easiest time to act.

Telling mice from other animals.

Size and timing separate most cases. Mice are light and quick, and they work at night. Squirrels are heavier and active by day. Birds flutter and chirp near vents at dawn. Raccoons, which this site does not cover, thump and drag at dusk and dawn.

If your description does not fit a mouse or a rat, say so on the call. The dispatcher will tell you honestly whether we are the right company for the animal.

Dust and droppings: ground rules first

A few minutes of reading now keeps dust out of the air later. The points follow the agency's own sequence.

  • Never dry-sweep, dry-vacuum or blow waste around with a leaf blower. Stirred dust is how people get exposed.
  • Give a closed room time to breathe: open doors and windows, then stay out for about half an hour.
  • Disposable gloves of rubber, vinyl or latex belong on your hands for anything soiled, and a thorough soap-and-water wash belongs at the end.
  • Drench droppings and nests, and the surface under them, with disinfectant or a bleach mix. Let it work for about five minutes.
  • Lift a dead rodent into a bag with a gloved hand or a tool, then seal it together with the used towels before the bin.
  • Fever, aches or shortness of breath within a few weeks of cleaning calls for a doctor. Say what you handled. A pest company cannot advise on health.

Still curious?

What do mice sound like in an attic?

Light scratching, quick patter and sometimes squeaking. The sounds are often at night and move from place to place.

What do rats sound like?

Heavier steps, scrabbling and occasional thumps. Rat sounds tend to be louder than mouse sounds.

Could it be the house settling?

Yes. Wood and metal expand and contract with temperature, causing pops and ticks, especially in cold weather.

Should I go into the attic?

Only if the space is safe and you follow the safety steps. Many homeowners prefer that a technician goes in.

Can squirrels or birds make these sounds?

Yes. Daytime noise and larger thumps may point to another animal. Tell the dispatcher what time you hear it.
