What makes a vehicle attractive.

An engine bay stays warm after a drive, sits out of the weather and is full of soft insulation. A vehicle left for weeks looks like shelter to a mouse or rat. A stored camper or trailer looks better still, because the cabin holds fabric, paper and crumbs.

Parking beside brush, a wood stack, an open field or a creek edge raises the odds. A garage that already has rodents is another common source.

Inspection points.

  • The engine bay: air box, wiring harness, battery area and firewall insulation.
  • The cabin air filter and the vent path to the dashboard.
  • The trunk, spare tire well and anything stored there.
  • The underside, for nests in frame cavities.
  • The parking spot, for the gaps and food that drew the animal.

If chewed wiring turns up, we show you and advise a mechanic. We do not do electrical work.

Clearing a nest safely.

Dry nest material and droppings can hold dust you should not breathe. We wear gloves and a respirator, mist the area with disinfectant and lift material out with wet paper towels. The waste is bagged and removed.

Compressed air and dry vacuums stay out of it because they spread dust. If you would rather clean the vehicle yourself, the CDC steps in the safety box below apply.

Keeping the next one out.

Take away the food. A bag of seed in the trunk or crumbs on the seat will bring animals back. Park away from brush and wood stacks when possible, and check the garage for gaps with the garage and basement service.

For a vehicle in long storage, look it over every couple of weeks. A short peek under the hood catches a nest before it becomes a repair bill.

What to expect.

A vehicle inspection often takes under an hour. If the vehicle will not start or sits in a storage lot, let the dispatcher know so the technician can plan. If animals live in your garage, trapping may be needed to stop repeat visits.

Until a technician arrives.

  • Seal up food tonight, pet kibble and seed included.
  • Let any trap you already placed stay exactly where it sits.
  • Keep bare hands out of the dark corners of an attic or crawl space.
  • Notice which outside door or window gets used most after sunset.

Dust and droppings: ground rules first

Everything below restates federal public health advice, so it applies whether or not you ever book a visit.

  • Skip brooms and vacuums entirely. They throw dry droppings into the air you breathe, so they stay in the closet.
  • Open what you can, walk outside and wait roughly thirty minutes while stale air clears.
  • Disposable gloves of rubber, vinyl or latex belong on your hands for anything soiled, and a thorough soap-and-water wash belongs at the end.
  • Soak the mess through and leave it around five minutes, so the dust cannot rise.
  • Take up the wet waste with paper towels, drop them in a plastic bag along with any dead animal, tie it shut and put it in the trash.
  • Fever, aches or shortness of breath within a few weeks of cleaning calls for a doctor. Say what you handled. A pest company cannot advise on health.

Vehicles: what people ask.

Will a mouse chew my wiring?

It might. Rodents gnaw wire insulation, and a chewed harness can cause starting trouble or warning lights. We report what we see, and a mechanic or electrician makes the repair.

Do you work on trailers and campers?

Yes, if the unit is parked somewhere we can reach. Campers and trailers often stand closed for months, which makes them appealing.

Is the smell a health matter?

Droppings and nest material can be a concern. Never blast them with compressed air. Follow the wet-cleaning steps in the safety box below.

Do mothballs work?

Not reliably, and the fumes are unpleasant inside a vehicle. Removing food and sealing the parking area works better.

Should I leave the hood up?

Some owners do while parked, since rodents like darkness. It costs nothing and offers no guarantee.
